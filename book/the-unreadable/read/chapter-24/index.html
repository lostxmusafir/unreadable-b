Chapter 24

Everything Seems Possible

✦

The months passed, and Priya's residency moved into its second year. They were, by all accounts, a successful couple�in love, ambitious, building a life together. Friends envied them. Families celebrated them. The future they had planned seemed not just possible, but inevitable.

Arjun's second book was published, a novel this time, drawing even more acclaim. He was interviewed by magazines, quoted in articles, invited to literary festivals across the country. His name was becoming known.

Priya, too, was rising. Her work in the hospital's emergency department had earned her a reputation for calm under pressure, for compassion without compromise. Senior doctors mentored her, patients requested her, colleagues respected her.

They were, in the words of a mutual friend, "relationship goals." And they believed it themselves.

But beneath the surface success, the cracks were widening. Arjun's secret�the thread, the phantom bride, the lie of omission�was a weight he carried daily. Priya's unspoken question�the doubt Raj had planted�was a seed that had taken root.

Neither spoke of these things. Both pretended. Both loved. Both believed, against the evidence, that their love was enough to hold the structure together.

It was enough. For now. But "for now" is a fragile foundation, and time, as it always does, was running out.

* * *

There was a period�a golden period, in retrospect�when everything seemed possible. When the love was strong, the future was bright, and the shadows had not yet lengthened into darkness.

It was the winter of Priya's second residency year. They took a short vacation�their first real holiday together, away from the city, away from the hospital, away from the pressures of their lives.

They went to Goa, to a quiet beach away from the tourist crowds. They swam in the warm sea, walked on the sand at dusk, ate fresh fish at a shack by the water. They were, for those few days, utterly free.

On their last evening, they sat on the beach watching the sun melt into the Arabian Sea. Priya rested her head on Arjun's shoulder, and they said nothing for a long time.

"I'm happy," Priya said finally. "Truly happy."

"Me too," Arjun said. And he was. In that moment, with the sea before them and the woman he loved beside him, he was happy in a way that felt complete.

The thread was silent. Raj was forgotten. The doubts were quiet. There was only the present, perfect and sufficient.

"When we get back," Priya said, "we should start planning the wedding. For real. A date, a venue, all of it."

"Yes," Arjun said. "Let's do it. Let's make it real."

And they meant it. They meant every word. The wedding would happen. The life would happen. Everything, in that golden moment, seemed not just possible but certain.

They could not know that the very act of planning�of making the future concrete�would trigger the forces that would tear them apart.

They returned from Goa with sand in their clothes and plans in their hearts. The wedding date was set�six months hence, in the winter, at a venue in Mumbai that Priya's mother had already begun scouting.

Arjun told his parents. He told his grandparents, in a letter that spoke of Priya with a tenderness that even Rama, with all his beliefs about destiny, could not fault. He did not mention the thread. He did not mention the phantom bride. He spoke only of the woman he loved and the life they would build.

Rama wrote back, his letter characteristically cryptic: "I am glad you have found love, my boy. But remember�the thread is not a thing we can simply set aside. It is a part of who you are. Honor it, even as you honor your choice."

Arjun read the words and felt a flicker of unease. Honor the thread? How? By telling Priya? By acknowledging the phantom bride? He did not know what his grandfather meant, and he did not want to find out.

He folded the letter and put it away, in the drawer where he kept the stones of his silence.

The wedding plans proceeded. Invitations were drafted. Caterers were consulted. Priya's mother took over with the efficiency of a general, and Arjun's mother deferred to her with the grace of someone relieved of duty.

Everything seemed possible. Everything seemed certain. Everything seemed, at last, to be falling into place.

It was the last moment of certainty they would have for a very long time.

That night, Arjun stood on the balcony of their apartment, looking out at the city lights. The wedding was six months away. Priya was asleep inside, the woman he would marry, the love of his life.

The thread pulsed once, faintly, like a distant heartbeat. He ignored it, as he had learned to do.

He thought of his grandfather's letter: "Honor the thread, even as you honor your choice."

Perhaps, he thought, honoring the thread simply meant accepting that it had been part of his journey�a childhood belief that had led him, somehow, to this moment, to this woman, to this life. Perhaps the thread had not been a chain, but a road. A road that had brought him to Priya.

It was a comforting thought. He held onto it as he went to bed, as he lay beside Priya, as he fell asleep with her hand in his.

Everything seemed possible. The future was bright. The love was real.

And in the space between sleep and waking, the phantom bride stood at the edge of his dream, watching, waiting, patient as ever�for the thread, unlike human love, does not expire, does not tire, does not forget.
