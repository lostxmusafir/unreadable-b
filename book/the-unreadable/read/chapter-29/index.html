Chapter 29

The Unraveling

✦

Raj, sensing the fracture was near breaking, made his final move. He sent Priya a message�not about Arjun this time, but about herself.

"You deserve certainty, Priya. A man whose heart is fully yours. Not someone who carries a thread to a ghost. You're a catch�a doctor, beautiful, kind. Don't settle for half a man."

The word "half" landed like a stone. Half a man. Because of the thread. Because of the door he kept locked.

Priya did not respond. But the word echoed. Half. Was Arjun half hers? Was there another half, tied to a destiny she could not see?

She thought of the day he had proposed on the hill in Lonavala, of the promise in his eyes, of the certainty she had felt. Had that certainty been built on a lie? Had he been carrying the thread even then, even as he spoke of their future?

She did not know. And the not-knowing, Raj understood, was more destructive than any lie he could tell.

Arjun, oblivious to the message, noticed only that Priya had grown colder still. "What now?" he asked, weary.

"Nothing," she said. "Just thinking."

She was thinking about whether a love built on a hidden truth could survive. And she was beginning to doubt that it could.

* * *

The unraveling of a relationship is rarely a single event. It is a thousand small surrenders, a million tiny choices to protect oneself rather than the other, an accumulation of moments when two people decide, silently, to stop trying.

For Arjun and Priya, the unraveling accelerated when Priya, overwhelmed by doubt, did something she would later regret. She told her mother she wanted to postpone the wedding indefinitely.

The words, once spoken, could not be unsaid. Arjun's family learned of the postponement through a third party�Priya's mother mentioned it to a relative, who mentioned it to Arjun's aunt, who told his mother. The gossip traveled faster than any direct conversation could have.

Arjun's mother, wounded and angry, confronted Priya. "You're calling it off? After everything? Without even telling Arjun to his face?"

"I didn't call it off," Priya said, defensive. "I said postpone. Until we sort things out."

"Same thing," the older woman said, and left.

Arjun, when he heard, felt a rage he had never known. Not at Priya�though he was angry�but at the whole situation, at Raj, at the families, at the thread that had somehow engineered all of this.

"You postponed the wedding?" he said, when they were alone. "Without telling me?"

"I told my mother. I was going to tell you. But then your aunt called mine, and�"

"You went to your mother before you came to me? About our wedding?"

"I needed to talk to someone, Arjun! You wouldn't talk to me! You locked the door!"

"And you ran to your mother like we're children!"

It was the worst fight they had ever had. Not because of the words, but because of what the words revealed: that neither trusted the other to hold their pain, that both had already begun to imagine a life apart.

In the aftermath, they stopped sleeping in the same bed. Arjun took the couch. Priya stayed in the bedroom. The apartment, once a home they were building, became a temporary arrangement�a waiting room for a decision neither could make.

The thread pulsed constantly now, no longer a background hum but a insistent thrum. Arjun felt it and wondered, in his darkest moments, if it was right. If the destiny it pointed to was the real one, and Priya the deviation.

These thoughts horrified him. He loved Priya. He had chosen her. But the thread, awakened by the crisis, was making its case again�whispering that the love he had built was a rebellion against his true fate.

He hated the thread in those moments. Hated his grandfather for planting it. Hated himself for never fully uprooting it.

Priya, in the bedroom, cried quietly into her pillow. She did not know about the thread's resurgence. She only knew that the man she loved felt distant, unreachable, somehow absent even when present.

She considered leaving. Not permanently�she was not ready for that�but for a few days, to clear her head. She mentioned it to a friend, who advised caution. "Don't make decisions when you're hurt. Wait. Heal. Then decide."

She waited. But waiting, in a fractured relationship, is not healing. It is simply the prolonging of the fracture.

The external world, oblivious to their private collapse, continued to celebrate them. Arjun's book launch was attended by well-wishers who praised the "beautiful love story" woven through his prose. Priya was commended at the hospital for her dedication. To everyone else, they were fine.

It is a peculiar cruelty of modern life that the more a couple appears successful, the less permission they have to fail. Arjun and Priya wore their masks well�smiling at events, posting cheerful updates, performing the roles of the happy couple for an audience that needed them to be happy.

But the performance exhausted them. Each public smile was a private wound. Each "we're great" was a silent "we're lost."

Arjun, in a moment of rare honesty with a friend, said: "I love her. I've never loved anyone else. But I don't know if we can get back from this."

"Then tell her," the friend said. "Tell her everything. The thread, the doubt, all of it. She can't fight a ghost she can't see."

Arjun knew the friend was right. But the door, locked for so long, had rusted shut. He no longer knew how to open it.

And Priya, asked the same question by her own friend, said: "I love him. But I'm tired of loving someone who won't let me all the way in. I can't be the only one reaching across."

Neither reached. Both waited. The glass wall thickened to stone.
